\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
This study examines whether macroeconomic covariates improve the practical value of zero-shot time-series foundation-model forecasts in portfolio construction. The central question is not only whether external variables reduce forecast error, but whether any statistical improvement survives the nonlinear transformation from forecasts to portfolio weights and ultimately produces economically better investment outcomes. A controlled predict-then-optimize experiment is implemented with Chronos-2 on 11 U.S. sector exchange-traded funds over a 2023--2026 evaluation period. Three forecasting arms are compared: a price-only baseline (Arm B), a model supplied with historical macroeconomic covariates (Arm A), and an extension that first forecasts future macroeconomic paths and supplies them as future covariates (Arm C). The macro variables are VIX volatility, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, WTI crude oil, gold, and the U.S. dollar index. Forecasts use information available through each decision date and predict the next 21 trading days; the portfolio holding horizon is aligned to the same 21-day interval. Historical covariates improve several forecast-error measures relative to the no-covariate baseline. In the portfolio experiment, Arm A achieves a cumulative return of 65.36\% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.098, compared with 44.92\% and 0.839 for Arm B. However, the paired circular block-bootstrap confidence interval for the Sharpe difference includes zero (difference 0.264; 95\% CI [-0.202, 0.735]; p=0.2396), so the economic improvement is not statistically conclusive.

\textbf{Keywords:} time-series foundation models, Chronos-2, macroeconomic covariates, portfolio optimization, zero-shot forecasting, sector ETFs, Sharpe ratio

\chapter{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\section{Motivation}\label{motivation}

Forecasting financial markets is difficult because the signal of
interest is weak relative to noise, relationships change across market
regimes, and a model that performs well on a statistical loss does not
necessarily create value after forecasts are translated into investment
decisions. These difficulties make financial forecasting a demanding
test for modern machine-learning systems. At the same time, the
emergence of time-series foundation models has changed the practical
forecasting workflow. Instead of training a new model for every asset
and horizon, a pretrained model can be applied zero-shot to previously
unseen series. Chronos and its successor Chronos-2 are examples of this
paradigm. Chronos-2 extends the foundation-model idea from univariate
forecasting to multivariate and covariate-informed forecasting, allowing
external variables to enter the model without task-specific retraining.

The possibility of conditioning a pretrained forecaster on external
information is particularly relevant for finance. Asset prices do not
evolve in isolation. Equity sectors can respond differently to
volatility, interest rates, commodity prices, and currency conditions.
Energy-related sectors may be sensitive to oil; rate-sensitive sectors
can react to changes in Treasury yields; the VIX can proxy market-wide
risk aversion; gold can reflect defensive demand and inflation concerns;
and the U.S. dollar can affect multinational firms and global financial
conditions. A sufficiently flexible nonlinear forecaster may be able to
use these variables in ways that simple linear predictive regressions
cannot.

Yet a forecasting improvement is not the same as an investment
improvement. A portfolio optimizer acts on relative expected returns,
risk estimates, constraints, and transaction costs. Small forecast
changes can produce large reallocations when they alter the
cross-sectional ranking of assets, while a lower average forecast error
may have little portfolio effect if it occurs on observations that do
not change portfolio weights. Conversely, a statistically modest
improvement can matter economically when it consistently improves the
ranking of investable assets. This distinction motivates the present
study.

\section{Problem Statement and Research
Questions}\label{problem-statement-and-research-questions}

Prior research provides two pieces of evidence that are individually
important but not yet fully connected. First, covariates can improve
forecasting when the model is sufficiently flexible. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu
(2020), for example, show that nonlinear machine-learning methods can
exploit rich predictor interactions and generate economic gains in
asset-pricing applications. More recently, ChronosX demonstrates that
pretrained time-series models can be adapted to use exogenous variables,
and Chronos-2 directly supports past and future covariates. Second,
recent studies show that foundation-model forecasts can be incorporated
into financial strategies, including long/short stock portfolios,
factor-selection systems, and portfolio reinforcement learning. The
missing link is a controlled test of whether adding macroeconomic
covariates to a zero-shot foundation model changes not only forecast
accuracy but also the value of a multi-asset portfolio.

The main research question is therefore: Do macroeconomic covariates
improve the economic value of zero-shot Chronos-2 forecasts when those
forecasts are used in a fixed portfolio-construction pipeline?

This question is decomposed into four empirical sub-questions. RQ1 asks
whether historical macroeconomic covariates improve out-of-sample
forecast accuracy relative to a price-only Chronos-2 baseline. RQ2 asks
whether any forecast improvement translates into higher portfolio return
or risk-adjusted performance under the same mean-variance allocation
rule. RQ3 asks whether explicitly forecasting the future macroeconomic
path and providing it as future covariates adds value beyond historical
covariates alone. RQ4 asks which individual covariates appear most
important for the observed portfolio results.

\section{Contributions}\label{contributions}

The study makes four practical contributions. First, it constructs a
controlled on/off covariate experiment in which the core forecasting
model and portfolio rule are held fixed. This isolates the role of the
information set more cleanly than comparisons across different
forecasting architectures. Second, it aligns the information cutoff,
forecast horizon, expected-return definition, and realized holding
period. At each decision date t, the model sees information available
through t, forecasts t+1 through t+21, and the portfolio is evaluated
over exactly the same 21-trading-day interval. Third, the evaluation
combines conventional forecast metrics with return-oriented measures,
cross-sectional rank information, portfolio outcomes, and
time-series-aware bootstrap inference. Fourth, the
leave-one-covariate-out experiment examines whether the aggregate effect
of covariates is concentrated in particular macro variables.

The contribution is deliberately narrower than claiming a new
forecasting architecture. Chronos-2 is used as a frozen pretrained
model, and the portfolio optimizer is a conventional long-only
mean-variance rule. The novelty lies in connecting covariate-informed
zero-shot forecasting to multi-asset economic evaluation under a
controlled and reproducible design.

\chapter{Related Work}\label{related-work}

\section{Return Predictability and Macroeconomic Information}
\label{return-predictability-and-macroeconomic-information}

Predicting asset returns is a key challenge in finance. 
Traditional methods look at whether financial and macroeconomic variables 
can help forecast future returns. Common predictors include valuation ratios 
such as dividend-to-price and earnings-to-price, the book-to-market ratio, 
equity issuance, interest rates, term and default spreads, inflation, and 
consumption-related variables (Goyal and Welch, 2008). Researchers typically 
use these factors in predictive regressions that link future excess market returns to information from the previous period. The main idea is that shifts in financial conditions and the broader economy may point to changes in expected returns.

Although some patterns appear in historical data, these relationships often fail to produce strong real-time forecasts. Goyal and Welch (2008) examined this by testing predictive variables as an investor who had only current information. They built forecasting models step by step and compared their out-of-sample predictions to a simple benchmark: the historical average of the equity premium. Their results showed a big difference between how well models explained past data and how well they predicted new data. Many variables that once seemed useful for predicting returns did not consistently beat the historical average, and results changed depending on the time period and data used. For example, out of 51 annual predictive regressions, 46 had higher root mean squared errors than the mean forecast. This highlights how hard it is to turn statistical links from past data into reliable real-time forecasts.

One major drawback of traditional predictive regressions is their limited set of models. Financial markets have many possible predictors, and many of them are closely related. The links between these predictors and future returns can be nonlinear or depend on how variables interact. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) suggest that these challenges make asset pricing well suited to machine-learning methods. Machine-learning models can handle many predictors by using regularization, variable selection, and dimension reduction. They can also find nonlinear relationships and interactions that are hard to capture with standard linear regressions.



Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) evaluated many methods, such as penalized linear models, principal component and partial least-squares regressions, regression trees, boosted trees, random forests, and neural networks. Their analysis found that machine-learning forecasts can offer real economic benefits compared to traditional regression methods, especially with regression trees and neural networks. These gains come from machine-learning models' ability to find nonlinear links between predictors that simpler models might miss. However, this flexibility can also lead to overfitting. To address this, machine-learning methods use regularization and model selection to improve performance on new data.

Overall, this research shows how financial return forecasting has changed over time. Early studies focused on finding single financial or macroeconomic variables that could predict returns. More recent work looks at whether flexible models can use information from many predictors. The weak out-of-sample results found by Goyal and Welch (2008) set an important standard: test predictive relationships on data not used to build the model, not just on in-sample fit. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) also show that nonlinear models can find interactions between predictors that standard methods might miss. Still, these methods require models built specifically to predict returns. This raises a new question: can modern pretrained time-series models use financial and macroeconomic data without needing to be tailored to each task? This is the start of the next stage of the literature.


\section{Machine Learning and Nonlinear Return Prediction}
\label{machine-learning-and-nonlinear-return-prediction}

The weak out-of-sample performance of many traditional return-prediction models does not necessarily mean financial and macroeconomic predictors lack informative value. Rather, the relationship between predictors and future returns may be too complex for simple linear models to capture effectively. Financial variables often interact, and their effects may depend on other variables. Consequently, model flexibility is essential when evaluating the usefulness of additional information.

Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) address this issue by applying machine learning techniques to empirical asset pricing. They highlight two features of return prediction that underline the relevance of machine learning. First, the set of potentially useful predictors is often large, with many predictors displaying strong correlations. Second, the functional relationship between these predictors and expected returns is typically unknown. These factors challenge conventional regression models, especially when interactions and nonlinear relationships are significant.

Machine learning methods deliver several solutions to these problems. Techniques such as regularization and dimension reduction manage the complexity of large predictor sets. Neural networks and Regression trees can depict nonlinear relationships and interactions without specifying every functional form in advance. This flexibility is especially valuable when the predictive effect of one variable depends on the state of another, rather than remaining constant across all observations.


In their empirical analysis, Gu, Kelly, and Xiu compare multiple predictive approaches and find that methods capable of modeling nonlinear relationships, particularly neural networks and tree-based models, perform well in return prediction. Their findings show that interactions among predictors notably contribute to this performance. This implies that the value of financial information depends on the model used to process it. A predictor that seems weak in a restrictive model may still provide valuable information when combined with other variables in a more flexible predictive framework.

However, increased model flexibility also raises the risk of fitting patterns that fail to generalize to new data. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu therefore stress the importance of out-of-sample evaluation and adopt techniques such as regularization and model selection to reduce overfitting. This approach is especially essential in financial forecasting, where relationships identified in historical data may shift over time and strong in-sample performance does not ensure future forecasting performance.

The study further shows that return forecasts should be evaluated in economic terms, not solely by statistical prediction metrics. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu use out-of-sample forecasts to construct portfolios, thereby linking predictive performance to investment outcomes. This distinction is significant because a statistically superior forecast is economically valuable only if it informs investment decisions in a meaningful way.

The machine-learning literature therefore represents a key development bridging conventional predictive regressions and newer foundation-model approaches. It shows that flexible models can capture nonlinear effects and interactions across large sets of financial predictors, while stressing the need for rigorous out-of-sample evaluation. Nevertheless, these machine-learning models are typically trained for specific prediction tasks. An open question is whether a model pretrained on large collections of time series can transfer its forecasting skills to previously unseen financial series without additional task-specific training. This question motivates the exploration of time-series foundation models, which will be discussed in the next section.





\section{Time-Series Foundation
Models and covariates Forecasting}\label{time-series-foundation-models and covariates forecasting}

In recent years, time-series forecasting has shifted away from case-by-case models toward pretrained foundation models. These models are trained on large numbers of time series and are intended to be able to apply their forecasting abilities to data that has not been seen before. A key aspect is zero-shot forecasting, which lets a pretrained model predict a new time series without being trained specifically on that downstream dataset.

Chronos is a good example of this method. Instead of using the original time-series values, it scales and quantizes them, then maps the results to a fixed vocabulary of discrete tokens. The tokens can then be handled using transformer architectures initially created for language modeling. The model learns a probability distribution over possible future tokens, which can then be converted back into numerical values to produce probabilistic forecasts. This approach enables Chronos to apply the knowledge gained during pretraining to time series that it has not seen before without the need for conventional task-specific model estimation.



The ability of pretrained models to forecast without prior training changes the usual forecasting workflow. Traditional statistical and machine-learning methods usually estimate parameters from observations of the specific dataset being studied. A pretrained model, by contrast, can start with the forecasting knowledge it has acquired from a wide variety of time series. Because of this, foundation models are attractive when many individual series need forecasting or when repeatedly estimating separate forecasting models would be costly.

The original Chronos formulation focuses on a single variable; its forecasts rely on the target series' historical values and do not naturally include external covariates. This is a significant limitation in many real-world forecasting situations, since a target variable may be influenced by information not found in its own historical values. For instance, in economic and financial applications, market conditions, interest rates, volatility, commodity prices, and other variables can provide extra information about the environment in which the target series develops.

To address this limitation, ChronosX extends pretrained time-series models to include exogenous variables. Rather than replacing the pretrained model, it uses lightweight adapter modules which introduce the covariate information at various stages of the forecasting process. The Input Injection Block incorporates covariates observed during the historical period by altering the pretrained token embeddings. The Output Injection Block accounts for future covariates by modifying the model's output logits, thereby changing its predictive distribution. Depending on the forecasting problem, the framework can use past covariates, future covariates, or both.

A key aspect of ChronosX is that the underlying pretrained model need not be fully fine-tuned; the original Chronos parameters can stay fixed while the new adapter modules are trained for the downstream forecasting task. The authors also examine a fully fine-tuned version, in which the pretrained parameters are updated alongside the additional components. ChronosX thus shows that a pretrained model can adapt to external information without retraining the entire foundation model.

The empirical analysis carried out on ChronosX examines this method using both synthetic and real-world datasets that include covariates. The study presents synthetic datasets which involve various types of external effects and also tests the method on actual forecasting datasets. The results indicate that the covariate-aware extensions yield better forecasting performance when compared to the corresponding pre-trained models which do not make use of this extra information. The study thus offers direct evidence that external covariates can contain useful information for pre-trained time-series forecasting.

Another development is adapting Chronos to handle multivariate forecasting. Multivariate-Chronos examines whether a pretrained forecasting model can use dependencies among multiple related time series rather than treating each series separately. This is especially important when several variables change jointly, since information in one series might help forecast another. The study examines financial applications involving series such as equities and interest rates and investigates whether cross-series information improves forecasting performance compared with the univariate approach.

Multivariate forecasting and covariate forecasting are conceptually distinct, although closely related. In the case of a multivariate approach, the extra information is mainly derived from other time series that are modeled together, while in covariate forecasting variables are introduced to provide contextual information regarding the target variable. For a financial asset, the prices or returns of other assets constitute the cross-series information, and variables indicating volatility, interest rates, commodities, currencies, or general economic conditions can act as external covariates. It is important to distinguish between these different kinds of information when assessing which type of additional information has an impact on the forecast.

Chronos, ChronosX, and Multivariate-Chronos thus correspond to successive developments which are directly pertinent to the current study. Chronos shows that pre-trained models can perform zero-shot forecasting on unseen time series. ChronosX shows that pre-trained models can be adapted to use explicit covariates, whereas Multivariate-Chronos shows the potential benefit of using information shared among related series. Taken together, these advances show that forecasting with foundation models can be improved by information beyond the historical values of the individual target series.

At the same time, a significant distinction remains relevant for financial applications. Just because extra information improves a forecasting metric does not mean the improvement will remain economically useful when the forecasts are turned into investment decisions. Moreover, an adapter-based method for incorporating covariates is not the same as assessing external information in a zero-shot forecasting scenario without carrying out downstream parameter training. This becomes especially important when foundation-model forecasts are applied to multiple financial assets, which is why the financial and portfolio applications discussed in the following section are relevant.


\section{Foundation Models in Finance and Portfolio
Construction}\label{foundation-models-in-finance-and-portfolio-construction}

Recent studies have been evaluating time-series foundation models for financial forecasting and portfolio management. This represents an important development in the forecasting literature since greater statistical accuracy does not always lead to economically worthwhile investment decisions. Financial returns are unpredictable, trading strategies involve costs, and the method used to translate forecasts into portfolio positions can substantially affect actual performance.

Noguer i Alonso and Franklin (2026) compare a number of pretrained time-series foundation models, such as Chronos, Chronos-2, TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2.0, and the various TimeGPT models, with neural networks that have been trained locally in order to forecast returns on U.S. equities. Their findings indicate that pretrained models can compete with models trained specifically on financial data, although improvements over simple financial benchmarks are generally minor. This underscores the difficulty of deriving economically meaningful predictability from asset returns even when using large pretrained models.

Valeyre and Aboura (2024) apply Chronos to forecast residual returns of U.S. stocks, then rank stocks based on these forecasts before forming cross-sectional long/short portfolios. Their findings indicate that zero-shot Chronos forecasts can generate economically relevant trading signals under certain specifications. Yet the reported performance is sensitive to both transaction costs and the choice of benchmark; for instance, a zero-shot specification based on PCA yields a gross Sharpe ratio of about 3.17, but this drops to about -1.49 when a trading cost of three basis points is taken into account. This shows that a high gross portfolio return does not mean that the forecasting signal is practically profitable.

Engel and others (2025) differ from earlier work in how they use foundation-model forecasts. Their approach combines conditional autoencoders with a factor-selection method that accounts for uncertainty in portfolio optimization. Chronos is used in a zero-shot scenario to produce probabilistic forecasts of latent factor returns, and the forecast uncertainty is used to decide which factors to include in the portfolio. The authors find improvements in several risk-adjusted performance measures when they select more predictable factors. The results indicate that foundation models can assist portfolio decisions not only by providing point forecasts but by using the uncertainty information present in their predictive distributions.

Other studies have incorporated foundation models into wider financial systems. In his 2026 study, Pishehvar includes a frozen Chronos module in a reinforcement-learning approach to personal portfolio management, showing that pretrained forecasting models can serve as components within more complex allocation systems. Likewise, recent multivariate financial applications have found that information from related series can improve foundation-model forecasts in some cases, even though including less relevant series can also reduce performance. This suggests that additional information is useful only when it provides relevant predictive context.

Overall, the literature indicates that foundation models can be applied to forecasting financial returns, ranking securities, selecting factors, developing trading strategies, and constructing portfolio-management systems. However, it also shows that improved forecasts do not necessarily lead to better portfolio results and that economic performance can depend heavily on transaction costs, the information given to the model, and the method used for downstream allocation. Existing studies have shown that foundation models can be used to make financial decisions, but they do not directly address whether adding macroeconomic covariates to an otherwise comparable zero-shot forecasting pipeline improves both forecast accuracy and multi-asset portfolio performance. This gap is the focus of the following section.


\section{Research Gap and Contribution}\label{research-gap and contribution}

The reviewed literature indicates that macroeconomic and financial variables may provide information relevant to return prediction, although their predictive utility often lacks stability out of sample. Recent machine-learning research shows that flexible models can capture nonlinear relationships among predictors, and time-series foundation models can transfer forecasting capabilities to previously unseen series without conventional task-specific training. (Masini et al., 2021, pp. 76-111)

Recent advancements in foundation models demonstrate that incorporating information beyond the historical target series can enhance forecasting. (Rahimikia et al., 2025) For example, ChronosX integrates exogenous variables using trainable adapter components, while Multivariate-Chronos leverages information shared across related time series. These studies suggest that additional context can improve foundation-model forecasting under specific conditions. (Rahimikia et al., 2025) However, they do not directly address whether macroeconomic information improves financial forecasts in a controlled zero-shot foundation-model setting or whether these improvements lead to better portfolio outcomes.

A parallel line of research applies foundation models to financial decision-making. Prior studies have utilized pretrained models for return forecasting, long/short trading strategies, uncertainty-aware factor selection, and comprehensive portfolio-management systems. These applications demonstrate that investment decisions can incorporate foundation-model forecasts. (Alonso \& Franklin, 2026) Nevertheless, variations in forecasting architectures, information sets, trading rules, and portfolio-construction procedures complicate efforts to isolate the incremental economic value attributable to external macroeconomic information. (Bagnara, 2022, pp. 27-56)

Our study addresses this gap by employing Chronos-2 and 11 U.S. sector ETFs in a controlled rolling experiment. The forecasting model and downstream portfolio-construction procedure remain constant, while the information provided to the forecaster varies systematically. A price-only specification serves as the baseline, whereas a covariate-enhanced specification incorporates historical data from five macro-financial variables: VIX volatility, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, WTI crude oil, gold, and the U.S. dollar index. This experimental design enables evaluation of the contribution of macroeconomic information without altering the forecasting architecture or allocation rule.

The experiment further investigates whether information regarding the future trajectory of macroeconomic variables offers additional value beyond historical observations. Since future macroeconomic values are unknown at the time of investment decisions, they are not sourced from realized future data. Instead, Chronos-2 forecasts the future path of each macroeconomic series and supplies these predicted values as future covariates for the ETF forecasts. This approach preserves the information available at each decision date and facilitates comparison between historical-covariate and forecasted-future-covariate scenarios.

The study evaluates the contribution of covariates at both the forecasting and portfolio levels. The study first assesses forecast performance to determine whether modifications to the information set improve forecast precision. The resulting forecasts are then processed through an identical portfolio-construction procedure to assess whether improved forecast quality leads to superior realized portfolio performance. This distinction is critical, as statistically improved forecasts do not always result in better investment outcomes.

A leave-one-covariate-out analysis examines the contribution of individual macroeconomic variables. The forecasting pipeline is repeated after removing one of the five covariates at a time, enabling assessment of whether the overall effect is broadly distributed or concentrated in specific variables. This analysis evaluates sensitivity and contribution, rather than establishing causal relationships.

This study does not propose a new foundation-model architecture or portfolio-optimization method. Instead, it offers a controlled, data-driven evaluation of whether macroeconomic information improves zero-shot foundation-model forecasting and whether that value persists through the transition from statistical forecasts to multi-asset portfolio decisions. By independently analyzing historical covariates, forecasted future covariates, portfolio outcomes, and individual-variable contributions while keeping the forecasting and allocation framework fixed, the study links covariate-aware foundation-model forecasting to the economic evaluation of financial forecasts.


\chapter{Methodology}\label{methodology}

\section{Research Design}\label{research-design}

3.1 Research Design

The empirical analysis makes use of a controlled comparison between different sets of information given to one pretrained time-series foundation model. Its main aim is to find out if including macroeconomic covariates leads to better forecasts of U.S. sector exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and whether these improvements result in better portfolio performance. In order to isolate the effect of the information set, the forecasting model, the forecast horizon, the method of evaluation, and the rule used for constructing portfolios are kept the same in all of the main experimental setups.

The pool of investable assets includes 11 ETFs that represent U.S. sectors. The forecasts are created by using the pretrained amazon/chronos-2 model without any task-specific fine-tuning. When at a given decision origin (t), the model is given only the information that was available up to that point and then generates forecasts for the next 21 trading days (from t+1 to t+21). The model makes use of a maximum historical context of 512 observations. The experiment runs from decision origins in January 2023 to July 2026, provided that a full 21-trading-day evaluation period is available.

The decision origins are defined as 21-trading-day intervals that do not overlap. At each origin, forecasts are produced, turned into expected returns, and then fed into the portfolio-construction procedure. The portfolio's performance is assessed over the same 21-trading-day interval. Each following decision origin comes after the previous holding period, which in this way avoids overlapping evaluation periods of the portfolio.

The study assesses three different forecasting methods. Arm B acts as the baseline by using only the historical series of the ETF target. Arm A builds on this by adding to the target history the historical macroeconomic covariates that were observed up to the date of the decision. Arm C includes in its analysis both the historical target and covariate data as does Arm A and also incorporates forecasts of the macroeconomic variables in the future over the 21-day period; these forecasts are produced by Chronos-2 on the basis of the information available by the decision date.

Hence, the experimental design changes the information given to the forecasting model while keeping the forecasting architecture constant. This method allows for a direct comparison between price-only forecasting, forecasting which takes into account historical macroeconomic data, and forecasting which incorporates both historical and projected future macroeconomic information.

\section{Forecasting Specifications}\label{forecasting-specifications}

Each of the three forecasting methods uses Chronos-2, with the same 21-trading-day prediction horizon and a maximum context window of 512 historical observations. The probabilistic forecasts are created at the 0.10, 0.50, and 0.90 quantiles; the model also calculates a predictive mean, even though the portfolio signal employed in the later allocation process is based on the median the \(q_{0.50}\) forecast at the conclusion of the 21-day horizon. (James \& Leung, 2026, pp. 2355-2378)

\subsection{Arm B: Price-only baseline.}
Arm B serves as the standard used for evaluating the value of macroeconomic information. For each ETF, Chronos-2 is given only the ETF's historical price series up to the decision date (t) and is not provided with any macroeconomic covariates. The model then produces a 21-step probabilistic forecast for each ETF.

\subsection{Arm A: Historical macroeconomic covariates.}
Arm A provides Chronos-2 with the same ETF target history, along with historical macroeconomic covariates available at (t). The target series and covariates are aligned on the common trading dates before the forecasting process. The macroeconomic variables thus provide additional historical context without using any realized information beyond the decision date. (Goetzmann et al., 2022)

\subsection{Arm C: Historical and forecasted future covariates.}
Arm C examines whether an estimated future macroeconomic path provides information beyond what historical covariates alone provide. At each decision point, Chronos-2 first forecasts each macroeconomic series independently for the next 21 trading days. The median (that is, the (\(q_{0.50}\))) forecast of each macroeconomic series is taken as its estimated future path. (Carriero et al., 2024) The ETF forecasts use the ETF's historical target series, the historical macroeconomic covariates, and these estimated future macroeconomic paths as future covariates.

The two-stage approach prevents the ETF forecaster from receiving actual future macroeconomic data. (Ansari et al., 2025) At the time of the decision (t), both the forecasts relating to the macroeconomy and the subsequent ETF forecasts are based only on the information available up to and including (t). (Ansari et al., 2025) Thus, Arm C assesses the value of forecasted future macroeconomic conditions rather than considering an impossible scenario in which future values of macroeconomic variables are known in advance.


For each ETF and decision origin, the forecasting stage saves the 0.10, 0.50, and 0.90 quantile forecasts, as well as the predictive mean, for each of the 21 forecast steps. (Gooijer, 2022) For the main forecast and portfolio evaluations, we use the terminal (t+21) prediction so the forecasting target matches the 21-trading-day portfolio holding period.

\section{Portfolio Construction}\label{portfolio-construction}

The expected returns derived from the forecasts at each decision origin are passed on to a common portfolio-construction procedure, and the allocation rule remains unchanged in all the forecasting arms so that any differences in the portfolio results are due to the forecasts rather than to changes in the portfolio strategy.

For ETF \(i\) at decision origin \(t\), the expected return over the 21-trading-day horizon is obtained from the terminal median price forecast:

\[
\hat{\mu}_{i,t}
=
\frac{\hat{P}^{(0.50)}_{i,t+21}}
     {P_{i,t}}
-1,
\]

The expected returns for all 11 sector ETFs make up the expected-return vector that is used in portfolio optimization, where \(P_{i,t}\) represents the observed ETF price at the decision date and \(\hat{P}^{(0.50)}_{i,t+21}\) is the median Chronos-2 forecast for the terminal price.

The portfolio risk is calculated using a trailing 252-trading-day covariance window based on historical ETF returns and then adjusted to the 21-trading-day investment horizon so risk estimates match the expected-return forecasts.

Portfolio weights are determined using constrained mean-variance optimization:

\[
\max_{\mathbf{w}}
\left[
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}
-
\frac{\gamma}{2}
\mathbf{w}^{\top}\Sigma_t\mathbf{w}
\right].
\]
The vector of portfolio weights is denoted by \(\mathbf{w}\), the vector of forecasted expected returns by \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{t}\), the covariance matrix is adjusted to a 21-trading-day horizon by \(\Sigma_t\), and \(\gamma\) stands for the risk-aversion parameter; this parameter is set at \(\gamma=5\).

The optimization is subject to the constraints.

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{11} w_i = 1,
\qquad
0 \leq w_i \leq 0.25.
\]

The portfolio is therefore both fully invested and long-only, with an upper limit of 25\% imposed on the amount invested in any single sector ETF. The method used to construct expected returns, the covariance-matrix estimator, the optimization objective, the risk-aversion parameter, and the portfolio constraints remain the same in Arms A, B, and C.




\section{Portfolio Evaluation}\label{portfolio-evaluation}

The portfolio's performance is assessed using the same non-overlapping periods of 21 trading days as were used in the forecasting experiment. At each decision origin, the optimized portfolio weights are applied to the realized ETF returns during the relevant holding period. Carrying out this process generates an out-of-sample sequence of portfolio returns for each forecasting arm.

Portfolio turnover between consecutive decision origins is calculated as
\[
\mathrm{Turnover}_{t}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left|w_{i,t}-w_{i,t-1}\right|.
\]

For the first portfolio period, the turnover is zero since there is no previous allocation. Transaction costs are assumed to be 10 basis points and are applied in proportion to turnover (Ruf \& Xie, n.d.):
\[
\mathrm{Cost}_{t}
=
\frac{10}{10{,}000}
\times
\mathrm{Turnover}_{t}.
\]

The net portfolio return is therefore
\[
r^{\mathrm{net}}_{p,t}
=
r^{\mathrm{gross}}_{p,t}
-
\mathrm{Cost}_{t}.
\]

The portfolio performance is presented in terms of total compounded return, annualized Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, and average turnover. The total return is obtained by compounding the net portfolio returns. Since each observation corresponds to a 21-trading-day holding period, the Sharpe ratio is annualized by using \(252/21\) periods in a year:
\[
\mathrm{Sharpe}
=
\sqrt{\frac{252}{21}}
\frac{\bar{r}_{p}}{s_{p}},
\]

where \(\bar{r}_{p}\) and \(s_{p}\) represent the mean and the sample standard deviation of the net 21-trading-day portfolio returns, respectively.

The maximum drawdown is determined by examining the cumulative wealth series and represents the largest percentage decline from a peak to a trough. Average turnover indicates the amount of portfolio reallocation across decision points.

In addition to the forecast-driven constrained mean-variance portfolio, we calculate an equal-weight portfolio as a common reference. Because equal weights do not depend on the forecasts, this reference portfolio is identical across Arms A, B, and C. The same transaction-cost and performance-evaluation framework is applied consistently across the portfolio results.



\section{Statistical Inference}\label{statistical-inference}

The differences in portfolio performance observed could be due to sampling variation and are thus subject to statistical inference. The analysis is concerned with the differences in the annualized Sharpe ratios of the forecast-driven constrained mean-variance portfolios MV\_CAP.

In order to maintain the correspondence between the portfolio returns produced by the different forecasting methods at the same decision points while at the same time keeping the short-range temporal dependence within the resampled blocks, a paired circular block-bootstrap procedure is applied, using a block length of three portfolio periods and 10,000 bootstrap replications.

In each bootstrap replication, we sample a starting position and then select consecutive blocks of paired portfolio-return observations using circular indexing until we have obtained a sample of the original data. We recalculate the annualized Sharpe ratio for both forecasting arms and record the difference between them . A 95\% percentile confidence interval and a two-sided bootstrap \(p\)-value for the difference in Sharpe ratios are then derived from the resulting empirical distribution.

Three pairwise comparisons are carried out. The comparison between Arm A and Arm B looks at whether historical macroeconomic covariates result in a change in risk-adjusted portfolio performance when compared with the price-only baseline. The comparison between Arm C and Arm B examines the specification which includes both historical and forecasted future macroeconomic information in relation to the baseline. Lastly, the comparison between Arm C and Arm A checks whether the forecasted future macroeconomic paths offer additional risk-adjusted portfolio value beyond what is provided by historical covariates alone.

The bootstrap method thus enhances the descriptive portfolio results by providing a measure of the uncertainty associated with the observed differences in Sharpe ratio. It is not sufficient simply to conclude that a higher sample Sharpe ratio indicates a reliable improvement in performance without also taking into account the relevant confidence interval and \(p\)-value.


\section{Covariate Ablation}\label{covariate-ablation}

An analysis that leaves out one covariate is carried out to assess the sensitivity of the historical covariate specification to individual macroeconomic variables. This analysis uses Arm A, since it provides Chronos-2 with historical ETF prices and the full set of historical macroeconomic covariates.

The forecasting experiment starts with the complete set of Arm A information and is then repeated each time one macroeconomic covariate is removed. The five variables involved are VIX volatility, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, WTI crude oil, gold, and the U.S. dollar index. Thus, each reduced specification differs from the full Arm by excluding one covariate.

We evaluate each leave-one-out specification using the same forecast metrics mentioned in Section~\ref{forecast-evaluation}: median MAPE, mean quantile loss, return MAE, return RMSE, directional accuracy, and mean rank IC. We then subject the resulting forecasts to the same portfolio-construction and evaluation procedure described in Sections~\ref{portfolio-construction} and \ref{portfolio-evaluation}. As a result, we obtain comparable total-return, Sharpe-ratio, maximum-drawdown, and turnover figures for both the full and reduced information sets.

By comparing each reduced specification with the full Arm A specification, we can tell whether the observed contribution of macroeconomic information is spread throughout the set of covariates or whether it is mainly dependent on certain individual variables.

The ablation results are seen as indications of sensitivity and of the incremental contribution rather than as evidence of causal effects; when a variable is removed it alters the information available to the forecasting model, but a subsequent change in the forecast or in portfolio performance does not prove that there is a causal relationship between that macroeconomic variable and ETF returns.

\chapter{Implementation and Experimental Setup}\label{implementation-and-experimental-setup}

\section{Data and Variables}\label{data-and-variables}

The investable universe contains 11 U.S. sector ETFs, providing a
cross-section that is broad enough to create meaningful allocation
choices while remaining interpretable. Daily adjusted price histories
are used for the forecast target and for realized portfolio returns. The
macroeconomic information set contains five variables: VIX volatility,
the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield, WTI crude oil, gold, and the U.S.
dollar index. These variables represent distinct channels of market
conditions: risk aversion, discount rates, commodity exposure, defensive
or inflation-sensitive demand, and currency/financial conditions.

The evaluation period covers decision origins from 2023 into 2026. The
exact rolling origins are generated from the available trading calendar
rather than assuming a fixed number of calendar days. Historical series
are aligned by date, and covariate gaps are handled using only
information available at the time. The final portfolio sample contains
42 non-overlapping 21-trading-day periods.

\section{Rolling Evaluation
Protocol}\label{rolling-evaluation-protocol}

At each origin, the pipeline performs the following sequence. First, the
target history and, where applicable, the historical macro covariates
are sliced through the decision date. Second, Chronos-2 generates a
21-step probabilistic forecast. For Arm C, a preliminary Chronos-2
forecast is generated for each macro series, and those predicted values
become the future-covariate path for the ETF forecast. Third, the
terminal horizon forecast is converted into an expected return relative
to P\_t. Fourth, the expected-return vector and trailing covariance
matrix are passed to the constrained optimizer. Fifth, the realized
portfolio return from t to t+21 is recorded after transaction costs. The
procedure then advances to the next non-overlapping origin.

This rolling design mimics a sequence of historical investment
decisions. No realized t+1 to t+21 ETF price or macro value is supplied
to the forecaster at time t. The only exception is the ex post
evaluation stage, where realized outcomes are compared with forecasts
after the simulated holding period has ended.

\section{Reproducibility and
Software}\label{reproducibility-and-software}

The project is implemented in Python and organized as a reproducible
repository. The revised experimental pipeline is stored under src\_v3,
while results\_v3 contains summary forecast metrics, portfolio
comparisons, significance tests, ablation summaries, and figures.
Chronos-2 is accessed through the chronos-forecasting package. Numerical
operations use NumPy and pandas, constrained optimization uses SciPy,
and figures use Matplotlib. The repository includes the input CSV files
and a requirements file so that a collaborator can clone the project and
reproduce the experiment.

The distinction between the original implementation and V3 is important.
The revised experiment was created to enforce consistent timing and
horizon definitions. The final paper therefore reports only V3 results.
Keeping the earlier implementation in the repository preserves
development history, but mixing its numerical outputs with V3 would
combine different experimental definitions and would not be
methodologically valid.

\section{Experimental Controls}\label{experimental-controls}

Several design choices are held fixed across forecasting arms. The model
checkpoint is identical, no arm is fine-tuned, the prediction horizon is
21 trading days, the covariance estimator is the same, the optimizer
uses the same risk-aversion parameter and weight constraints, and
transaction costs are applied identically. These controls are essential
because the research question concerns the marginal contribution of
covariate information rather than a competition among model
architectures.

Arm C is deliberately classified as an extension rather than part of the
main causal comparison. Its future covariates are themselves model
forecasts and therefore introduce an additional layer of estimation
error. If Arm C underperforms Arm A, that result does not imply that
true known-future covariates would be unhelpful; it implies that the
two-stage procedure of forecasting macro variables and then using those
forecasts as future covariates does not add value in this sample.

\section{Value of Forecasted Future Covariates}\label{value-of-forecasted-future-covariates}

Arm C aims to determine whether the estimated future macroeconomic paths provide information beyond the historical macroeconomic covariates already available to Arm A; the results offer mixed support for an incremental contribution.

In terms of forecasting, Arm C has the lowest return MAE, at 3.945\%, compared with 3.976\% for Arm A. It also achieves the highest mean rank IC, rising from 0.050 for Arm A to 0.107 for Arm C. The results show that the future-covariate specification provides useful information for some aspects of return forecasting, especially for the cross-sectional ranking of sector ETFs.

The improvement is not uniform across other forecast metrics; Arm C has a slightly higher median MAPE than Arm A (2.967\% vs. 2.957\%), and its return RMSE is 5.466\% compared with 5.297\% for Arm A. The mean quantile loss also increases, rising from 1.262% for Arm A to 1.375% for Arm C. Directional accuracy increases from 49.57% to 51.95%, although it still falls short of the 53.90% obtained by the price-only baseline.

The portfolio comparison also shows no incremental improvement over Arm A: Arm C attains a total return of 63.46\% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.050, compared with 65.36\% and 1.098 for Arm A. Moreover, Arm C's maximum drawdown is greater, at -16.08\% rather than -12.76\% for Arm A.

The bootstrap analysis comparing C and A shows a difference in Sharpe ratios of -0.048, a 95\% confidence interval of [-0.426, 0.413], and a two-sided (p)-value of 0.836. Therefore, the experiment yields no evidence that providing forecasted future macroeconomic paths improves risk-adjusted portfolio performance compared with the historical-covariate specification.

The findings thus indicate that forecasted future covariates can affect certain aspects of predictive performance, particularly cross-sectional ranking. Still, the extra information does not improve Arm A on the main portfolio outcomes observed in this experiment.

\section{Forecast Accuracy versus Economic
Value}\label{forecast-accuracy-versus-economic-value}

One of the key findings from the experiment is that there is no uniform relationship between the accuracy of statistical forecasts and portfolio value; the various forecasting units perform differently according to whether the assessment is made with respect to point errors, directional predictions, cross-sectional rankings, or optimized portfolio results.

Arm B is the best example of this. It has the highest directional accuracy, at 53.90\%,but yields the worst forecast-driven portfolio performance, with a total return of 44.92\% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.834. It therefore follows that more frequently correctly predicting the sign of individual ETF returns is not enough, in this sample, to produce the most effective optimized allocation.

Arm C also serves as an example since it attains the smallest return MAE, 3.945\%, and the highest mean rank IC, 0.107\%. Yet its portfolio Sharpe ratio of 1.050 is still lower than the 1.098 obtained by Arm A. On the other hand, Arm A has the lowest median MAPE, the lowest return RMSE, and the lowest mean quantile loss, along with delivering the most effective forecast-driven portfolio performance.

The results of the ablation analysis support this distinction: when VIX is removed, the MAE and RMSE of the returns increase while the portfolio's Sharpe ratio is slightly improved and the maximum drawdown is reduced; on the other hand, when WTI is removed, there are only relatively small changes in some of the forecast-error measures but a significant decline in total portfolio return and in the Sharpe ratio.

The fact that has been found shows that the quality of forecasts is multi-dimensional and that better results in a specific statistical error criterion do not have to lead directly to better investment performance. When constructing a portfolio it is necessary to take into account not only the absolute size of the forecast errors but also the expected returns relative to one another among the various assets, the way in which these returns interact with the estimated covariance matrix, the portfolio constraints, and the turnover and transaction costs that result.

Because of this, the findings indicate that financial forecasting models should be evaluated at both the predictive and the decision-making levels; forecast metrics show how closely the outputs of the models correspond to the actual results, while portfolio metrics examine whether the information in those forecasts is economically useful when a particular allocation rule is applied.

\section{Limitations and
Robustness}\label{limitations-and-robustness}

When drawing conclusions from the results, several limitations should be taken into account. The empirical comparison is based on 42 non-overlapping periods of portfolio performance. Even though the assessment covers a number of years and different market conditions, the number of independent periods for making portfolio decisions is still limited. As a result, the bootstrap confidence intervals for the differences in Sharpe ratios are relatively wide.

Second, the experiment looks at 11 U.S. sector ETFs. This offers a diversified range of sector exposures yet maintains a consistent investable universe across the forecasting sections, but the results cannot be automatically applied to individual equities, international markets, other asset classes, or to considerably larger investment universes.

Thirdly, the analysis adopts a forecasting and portfolio horizon of 21 trading days. The impact of the macroeconomic covariates could vary with a shorter or longer horizon. Moreover, portfolio results depend on the allocation framework employed, including the trailing covariance estimator, the fixed risk-aversion parameter, the long-only restriction, and the 25\% maximum asset weight. These options are deliberately kept unchanged to allow a controlled comparison of the different forecasting methods, although other portfolio specifications might lead to different economic outcomes.

Another source of endogeneity arises in the capture-covariate experiment. In Arm C, the future macroeconomic conditions are not observed but are instead forecasted first, after which these forecasted values are used as the future covariates. As a result, forecast errors can carry over to ETF forecasts, which may explain why Arm C improves some forecast dimensions without always delivering better portfolio performance than Arm A.

The leave-one-covariate-out analysis acts as an internal check on the make-up of the macroeconomic information set. It indicates that the portfolio results are not equally sensitive to each of the five variables and that eliminating WTI has the greatest negative impact on the observed total return and Sharpe ratio. Yet the ablation analysis does not prove causal importance and cannot take the place of robustness tests based on alternative datasets, time horizons, assumptions regarding transaction costs, or different portfolio-construction methods.

In the end, the bootstrap analysis offers an important qualification of the descriptive findings. Although Arms A and C have higher sample Sharpe ratios than Arm B, none of the differences in Sharpe ratios between the arms is statistically significant at conventional levels. It is therefore necessary to distinguish between the economic performance observed in this sample and evidence that the differences in performance are reliably different from zero.

When the results are considered as a whole, they offer evidence that macroeconomic covariates are able to modify both the characteristics of the forecasts and the subsequent portfolio results in a zero-shot Chronos-2 environment, although it also shows that the size and reliability of these effects vary according to the evaluation criterion. Before arriving at broader conclusions regarding generalizability, however, further robustness analysis would need to be carried out across different horizons, asset universes, allocation rules, and market periods.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

\section{Summary}\label{summary}

This research asked whether macroeconomic covariates improve the
economic value of zero-shot Chronos-2 forecasts in a multi-asset
portfolio setting. The study compared a price-only baseline with a
historical-covariate treatment and a future-covariate extension across
11 U.S. sector ETFs. The experimental design aligned the information
cutoff, 21-trading-day forecast horizon, expected-return definition, and
realized portfolio holding period, and held the portfolio optimizer
fixed across arms.

The results provide qualified support for the usefulness of historical
macroeconomic covariates. Arm A improves several forecast-error measures
relative to Arm B and produces a substantially stronger observed
mean-variance portfolio: 65.36\% cumulative return and a Sharpe ratio of
1.098 versus 44.92\% and 0.839. However, the block-bootstrap p-value of
0.2396 and a confidence interval spanning zero mean that the Sharpe
improvement is not statistically conclusive. The simple equal-weight
benchmark also remains superior in return and Sharpe ratio.

The future-covariate extension does not improve on historical covariates
alone. Arm C remains stronger than the no-covariate baseline in observed
portfolio performance but slightly weaker than Arm A. The ablation
analysis suggests that oil and gold are particularly associated with the
portfolio improvement, while the yield, dollar, and VIX effects are
smaller or depend on the metric considered.

\section{Implications}\label{implications}

The main implication is methodological. Foundation-model forecasting
should be evaluated at both the statistical and decision levels.
Covariates can change the economic behavior of a forecasting pipeline
even when average forecast metrics move only modestly. At the same time,
an economically attractive backtest difference should not be treated as
established evidence when uncertainty is large. Reporting forecast
errors, portfolio metrics, simple benchmarks, and formal inference
together produces a more credible assessment.

For practitioners, the results suggest that historical macro context can
be worth testing when using a flexible zero-shot forecaster across
related assets. But additional information should not be assumed to
help: noisy future covariate forecasts can fail to add value, and
individual covariates can have heterogeneous effects. The equal-weight
benchmark further shows that model sophistication does not guarantee
superior allocation.

\section{Future Work}\label{future-work}

Several extensions follow naturally. A longer historical evaluation
could improve statistical power and allow subperiod analysis across
market regimes. Alternative portfolio mappings could test whether robust
optimization, rank-based allocation, minimum-variance anchors, or
explicit turnover regularization better convert forecast information
into economic value. The covariate set could be expanded to include
credit, inflation, liquidity, and sector-specific variables.

A particularly important extension is decision-focused or end-to-end
training. The present study intentionally uses predict-then-optimize so
that the marginal role of covariates can be isolated. Recent Smart
Predict-then-Optimize research shows how forecasting models can instead
be trained against downstream portfolio objectives. Comparing the
controlled two-stage design with an end-to-end objective would test
whether covariate information becomes more useful when the model is
explicitly rewarded for decision quality rather than forecast accuracy.

Finally, future-covariate research should distinguish variables that are
truly known ahead of time from variables that must themselves be
forecast. Archived real-time macro expectations, futures curves,
scheduled events, and survey forecasts could provide more realistic
future information. Such work would clarify when
Chronos-2\textquotesingle s future-covariate interface offers genuine
financial value rather than simply adding another noisy prediction
layer.

\subsection{B.2 Interpretation Guide}\label{b.2-interpretation-guide}

For MAPE, quantile loss, return MAE, and return RMSE, lower values
indicate smaller errors. For directional accuracy and rank IC, higher
values indicate better sign or cross-sectional ordering. Portfolio total
return and Sharpe ratio are evaluated together with drawdown and
turnover; a higher return is not automatically preferable if it is
obtained with disproportionate risk or trading.
